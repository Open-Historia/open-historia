    Diplomacy

    Talking to other countries: leader voices, group threads, memory, reactions and unprompted approaches.

    Diplomacy is a conversation, not a menu. There is no "propose alliance" button and no accept/decline dialog. You open a chat with a country and talk its leader into something, or fail to.

    Find it in the 💬 Chat panel, Diplomacy tab, or from the popup when you click a country on the map.

    Talking to a country

    Threads you are part of. Germany opened this one unprompted after a time skip.

    Every country is played by its leader, in that leader's voice, shaped by their country's situation and by how they see you. Ask questions, make offers, issue threats, propose terms.

    What works is roughly what would work on a real government:

    • Offer something. A demand with nothing attached gets refused by anyone who can afford to.
    • Be concrete. "Let's cooperate" goes nowhere. "Preferential grain transit in exchange for your vote at the UN" is a proposal someone can answer.
    • Know who you are talking to. Read their dossier and ask for an intelligence briefing first. A country in crisis wants different things from a confident one.
    • Follow up. Agreements reached in conversation become real through subsequent events and orders, not the moment the words are said.

    Leaders remember

    Each country keeps its own private summary of its dealings with you, carried between conversations and fed back as context. A leader you threatened ten turns ago has not forgotten it, and neither has one you kept a promise to.

    This is separate from the world's event history. The event log records what happened; the diplomatic memory records how that country feels about you because of it.

    Reactions

    Leaders attach an emoji to your last message — a small, immediate read on how what you just said landed, before their actual reply arrives. It is a genuinely useful tell when you are probing.

    Group conversations

    Starting a thread. Select as many countries as you want in it — one for a private line, several for a summit.

    You can open a thread with several countries at once. Speaking passes around the participants in rotation, and the panel shows whose turn it is, with a Let X speak → control to hand the floor on.

    Group threads are where multi-party diplomacy actually happens: mediating between two rivals, assembling a coalition, or being ganged up on.

    They contact you

    Germany's opening message, in its government's voice and grounded in what the world had just done.

    Countries open conversations with you on their own initiative, in three ways:

    Because of an event. A border incident produces an ultimatum, a war scare produces an offer of mediation, a windfall produces a trade delegation. The other side always speaks first.

    As general outreach. Treaty feelers, non-aggression proposals, warnings, summit invitations — approaches that are not tied to any single event.

    Together these are capped at three per jump, and only when the approach genuinely serves the sender's interests. You will not be buried in spam.

    Unprompted, while you play. Roughly once a real-world minute, while the game is open and the tab is visible, there is a small chance some country simply writes to you. It never fires during a time skip, and a hidden tab never rolls.

    Unread threads are badged. Answering them is most of the game's texture — a campaign where you never reply is a much smaller campaign.

    The world cannot sign for you

    This is an explicit rule the simulation is held to: no other country can commit you to anything.

    The world may not sign treaties, alliances, ceasefires, surrenders, trade pacts or unions on your behalf, may not accept or reject offers for you, and may not have your country take landmark unilateral action — declaring war, ceding territory, changing government — unless it is directly executing an order you actually gave.

    When another country wants such a decision from you, it has to ask: as a chat where they speak first and make the proposal, or as an event describing the offer as open and awaiting your answer.

    So if you find yourself in an alliance, you agreed to it. Events remain free to narrate whatever other countries do among themselves.

    Turning talk into outcomes

    A conversation on its own changes attitudes. To change the world, pair it with an order:

    In chat: talk Germany into backing a joint force on the eastern border. In the queue: "Formalise the joint eastern deployment agreed with Berlin and begin stationing troops."

    The chat establishes that they are willing; the order is you acting on it. Doing only the first tends to produce a campaign where everyone likes you and nothing happens.

    Reading everyone else's diplomacy

    The Spy tab of the same panel shows conversations you are not part of, as far as your intelligence service can read them. That is a whole system of its own — see espionage.

    The beta diplomatic model

    Beta channel only. The stable release tracks relationships narratively — the world knows how countries feel because the events and conversations say so.

    The beta channel adds explicit ledgers: numeric relation scores between every pair of countries, a formal register of treaties and their status, and a war ledger that is the single authority on who is fighting whom. See relations, treaties and war.
