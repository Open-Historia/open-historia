Open Historia works differently from most strategy games. There is no build queue, no research
tree and no clock ticking down. You have one country, a world full of countries that a language
model plays, and a button that advances time.

## The loop

1. **Look at the world.** Click countries to read them. Ask your advisor what it thinks.
2. **Write down what you intend to do.** Orders go in the actions queue, in plain English.
3. **Talk to people.** Open a chat with any country and negotiate, threaten, or ask questions.
4. **Skip time.** Choose how far — six hours, a month, a year.
5. **Read what happened.** The world hands you back a set of events, revealed one at a time,
   with the map changing as each one lands.

Then you do it again. That is the whole game. Everything else is detail.

The important thing to internalise is that **step 4 is where the game happens**. Steps 1–3 are
you preparing; nothing moves until you skip time. If you sit and stare at the map, the world
sits and stares back.

## Your first ten minutes

Pick **Modern Day** — it is the built-in scenario and the most polished. Choose a country you
know something about; you will write better orders for a country whose situation you can
picture. Medium difficulty.

Then, before you do anything else:

- **Open the advisor** (🧭 on the right) and ask "what should I be worried about?" It reads your
  actual position and answers about it.
- **Click a neighbour** on the map. Read its panel. Ask for an intelligence briefing.
- **Open a chat** with someone and say something. They will answer in their leader's voice.

Now write one order — something modest, like *"begin talks with France about a trade
agreement"* — and skip forward **one month**. Read the events. That is the game.

## Writing orders that work

Orders are free text, and the model reads them as instructions from your head of state. The
difference between a good and a bad turn is usually the order, not the dice.

**Be specific about what and where.** *"Move troops"* gives the world nothing to work with.
*"Reinforce the eastern border with two infantry divisions and put the air force on alert"*
gives it a great deal.

**Say why, when it matters.** *"Raise tariffs on Chinese steel to protect domestic mills"*
produces a different chain of consequences than *"raise tariffs"*.

**Ask for things that take time.** A single month will not industrialise a country. Orders that
span turns get carried forward and referred back to.

**Don't write fiction.** You are giving orders, not narrating outcomes. *"Our army crushes
theirs and takes the capital"* is not an order — it is you trying to write the event. Say
*"launch an offensive toward the capital"* and let the world decide how it goes.

## Choosing how far to skip

| Skip | Good for |
|---|---|
| 6 hours – 3 days | An unfolding crisis, hour by hour. Ultimatums, coups, the opening of a war. |
| 1 week – 1 month | The normal pace. Enough happens to be interesting, not so much that you lose the thread. |
| 3 – 6 months | Quiet stretches, long build-ups, waiting for something to mature. |
| 1 year | Skipping ahead when nothing needs your attention. |

There is also **Auto-jump**, which lets the model choose the distance — it skips to the next
moment it thinks you would want to be present for.

Longer skips are not free: the world has to compress more into the same number of events, so
detail is lost. If something important is in motion, take smaller steps.

## Reading the results

Events arrive as a set and are revealed **one at a time**. The map rolls back to how it looked
before the jump and each event's changes are applied as you step through it — so you see the
border move, the unit appear, the government fall. The camera follows.

Use **Next event** to step through, or **Skip to end** to jump to the final state.

You can reopen the history at any time with the **«** button on the date pill.

## Made a mess of it?

**Undo turn** restores the world to how it was before the last time skip. It is right there in
the time panel. The game keeps several turns of history, so you can step back more than once.

This is not cheating — it is the main way people play. A time skip is a roll of the dice as much
as a decision, and rewinding a turn that went absurdly is normal.

For heavier intervention, the [cheats panel](/wiki/cheats/) lets you annex territory, edit
countries, author events by hand and switch which country you play.

## What the AI is and isn't doing

The model is not an opponent trying to beat you. It is playing every other country in the world
according to what those countries want, how they see you, and what has already happened. It also
narrates the consequences of your own orders.

That means:

- **It will not read your mind.** If your intent is only in your head, it does not exist.
- **It remembers.** Leaders recall previous conversations; the world refers back to earlier
  events.
- **Difficulty changes how it treats you**, not what it knows. See
  [starting a game](/wiki/new-game/) for what the six levels actually do.
