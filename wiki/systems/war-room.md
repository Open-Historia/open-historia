On beta the game fights its land wars itself. Where two countries at war hold land that touches,
a **battlefield** opens: hundreds of troops a side move over the real ground, and who holds what is
decided by where they go, not by a sentence in an event. The **War Room** is where you command
yours. On the stable build a land war is told by the time skip and moved by its formations: see
[military and combat](/wiki/military/).

## Battlefields

A battlefield opens by itself where the two sides of an active war hold land that touches. A war
fought overseas opens one too: when the sides do not touch and your land troops already stand on
the enemy's land, a battlefield opens where most of them are. Troops carried across the sea and put
ashore open one round the place they land. At most 12 battlefields are open in a world at once.

- **Troops.** Each side is given troops by its strength, up to 400. Your infantry, armour and
  artillery formations near the line join as troops and come back as formations, at the strength
  they ended with, when the battlefield closes. An army musters over weeks: not every troop is
  there on the first day.
- **Ground.** Each troop paints the ground it crosses in its side's colour. Ground cut off from
  every source of supply is filled the day the ring closes if it is empty and under 1,500 km²;
  anything else is a pocket, whose troops fight at a disadvantage, waste away and give up after ten
  days. A place under siege is shut in and holds out for 45 days. A region counts as taken once
  more than 60% of it is held, and changes colour on the map once all of it is. A capture the
  story writes for a region on a battlefield is left out: the troops take it or they do not. See
  [territory](/wiki/territory/).
- **Fought before it is told.** When you skip time, the period's land war is fought first, and the
  time skip's story is written from what happened, at no extra request.
- **Troops tire** as they march and fight and recover at rest. Fortified lines must be broken
  through. Weather and ground matter: rain, storms, mud, snow, blizzards, frost, heat and fog slow
  armies and blunt attacks, harsh winters wear down armies caught out in them, frozen lakes and seas
  can be crossed, and rough ground slows an advance.
- **In the air.** Every country at war flies its air arm over the battlefields it fights on. The
  side that holds the sky fights harder below and slows the other's columns. Yours fly in support of
  your troops; a commander the game runs may instead send its aircraft against your troops or keep
  them on the ground. Each side's aircraft are drawn circling over the battlefield.
- **Under a ceasefire** a battlefield stays, marked as such, and its fighting halts.

The **Fronts** feature switches all of this on or off for a scenario or a game, in the Features
tab. Off, land war is
the formations and the regions the story hands over, as on the stable build.

## Opening the War Room

While your troops are on a battlefield, a round red **War room** button with crossed swords stands
beside the toolbar, where the **🔍** search button usually is (search moves over; on a phone the
button stands above the toolbar). Press it. The War Room takes the whole screen: every other
control is put away, the map is fitted to the battlefield inside a steel frame whose top bar names
it, who fights whom and the date, and the panel stands on the right. The **×** in the panel's head
shuts it, and so does **Esc** with no tool in hand; the button beside it, **Show the whole
battlefield**, fits the map again. On a phone the panel fills the screen: **Map** folds it down to
show the map and the troop tools, and **Generals** brings it back.

**Nothing moves while the War Room is open.** Every order you give here is carried out when time
next passes. While a turn is being resolved, no orders are taken until it has landed.

If you fight on more than one battlefield, each has a tab across the top of the panel.

## The troop tools

The tools for your troops are on a rail down the left of the map. The number at its top is how many
troops you have picked out. A small plate beside the rail says what the tool in hand wants.

| Key | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Select** | Drag a box round your troops on the map to pick them. Hold Shift to add to those picked. |
| **Route** | Draw the way the troops picked out are to go. They follow it as the ground and the enemy allow, each moving by itself. |
| **Advance** | Send the troops picked out straight forward from where they stand, with no route. |
| **Dig in** | They entrench where they stand: stronger, but they do not move. |
| **Hold** | They keep their ground and attack nothing. |
| **Fall back** | They give ground toward their own rear. |
| **Release** | Give them back to their stretch of the line, under its general's orders. |
| **Withdraw** | They leave the battlefield and go home; with none picked out, all of yours do. The ground stays as it is. Once they are gone the key becomes **Send back**. |
| **Transport** | Carry the troops picked out by sea, by rail or by air to a place your side has no border with: choose the carrier, then click where. |
| **Diversion** | Draw a movement you want the other side to believe. |
| **Deploy** | Choose where your new troops on this battlefield come in, and call up fresh ones. |
| **Fortify** | Draw a fortified line to be built, then choose how strong. |
| **Fleets** | Command your fleets at sea. See [war at sea](/wiki/war-at-sea/). |
| **Supply** | Shade how well supplied the ground is. |

A small tab beside the rail tucks it against the edge and brings it out again.

**Routes into other countries.** A route that runs into a country not in this war is an attack on
it. The panel says so in amber on the route's row: when your troops reach its border it is at war
with you, and its land is fought over.

**Transport.** The menu lists only what carries troops in the game's year, with how many formations
each carrier takes and how far it goes in a day. One dispatch carries at most 40 formations. The
troops board when time next passes, and the panel shows the way found for them and how long it
takes, or why they cannot set out. Enemy fleets and aircraft along the way can catch a transporter.

**Diversions** work only through a spy of theirs you have turned: that agent sends them your
diversions in place of your plans. If you have turned none, there is nobody to show a diversion to,
and an agent of theirs you have not caught sends them your real plans. See
[espionage](/wiki/espionage/).

## Deploy: a front or a swarm

Fronts have not always existed. The **Deploy** tool asks which you want:

- **Front**: draw a line. Your new troops here come in along it, on ground your side holds near it.
- **Swarm**: click a place. Your new troops gather there as a group under one general of their
  own. Once deployed, a swarm's troops move and fight one by one like any others. New troops join
  the swarm while it is still near the place; once it has marched on, the next ones gather there as
  a new swarm under a new general.

**Formations to call up** says how many fresh formations the deployment calls up there when time
next passes, up to 60. Leave it empty for as many as a usual draft brings, or put 0 to only move
where new troops arrive. A call-up is limited by the men your country has left and the room along
the front, not by how large your army already is. Levies, drafts and reinforcements keep coming in
there afterwards.

The deployment's card under **Planned movements** says how many are coming, how many came and on
what day, or why none could be called up: no more men fit to serve, no room left on the
battlefield, no ground of your side's along the line, or the fighting halted. **Redraw** and
**Remove** are on the card.

Unless the scenario says otherwise, a war's troops are raised as swarms in games set before 1900
and along fronts from 1900 on. When the War Room opens, Deploy starts on the way you deployed there
last, or else on that.

## Fortified lines

**Fortify** draws a line you want built, over land your country holds; water may be crossed,
another country's land may not, not even a little way past the border. Once it is drawn, a plate
beside the rail asks how strong it is to be. Level 10, a line nobody passes, is for scenario
designers alone.

| Level | Name | An assault at two to one breaks through a stretch in about |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Palisade | 3 days |
| 4 | Earthworks | 6 days |
| 5 | Rampart | 10 days |
| 6 | Redoubts | 15 days |
| 7 | Bastions | 22 days |
| 8 | Fortress line | 32 days |
| 9 | Great wall | 50 days |

Siege guns do it in a third of the time. The plate gives the line's length, how long it takes to
build and the date it would stand from, reckoned from its length and level, your treasury and the
men you have, and what it costs of your war resources each month while it is built. Name it if you
wish, then **Confirm**, **Draw again** or **Cancel**. Your lines are listed in the panel, standing
or being built; **Stop building it** gives one up. A line holds nothing until it stands. A scenario
can switch fortified lines off, and then the key is greyed out.

## Supply

With **Supply** down, the map shades the ground: yours in amber is stretched past where supplies
come in full, yours in red is cut off, and pale ground is theirs, cut off. The plate gives each in
square kilometres. It shows once time has passed on the battlefield.

Your country's **war resources** pay for fortified lines while they are built, and its economy
refills them; a blockade slows that. Running low makes your troops recover more slowly and fight a
little worse. The figure is on your country's **⚔ Military** card.

## The panel

From top to bottom:

- **Your generals.** Each commands a body of your troops: how many formations at what strength,
  what they are doing and near where, and how many are cut off. Point at a general to light his
  troops on the map; **Pick** picks them out. His orders are listed under him in amber.
- **Weather ahead.** The weather of the next two weeks on this battlefield, a tile a day, then the
  spells and what each does to an army. It is the weather that will come, not a guess: time an
  offensive by it.
- **Planned movements.** Every movement your side means to make, drawn on the map as an arrow:
  your routes, your generals' orders, operations on your Projects board that send troops at a place
  here, and your diversions. A route or order has **Pick its troops** and **Call it off**. A Board
  operation can be redrawn (**Redraw**, **Suggested way**), held back (**Hold it back**, **Let it go
  ahead**), or sent again once it is over (**Send it again**). Under them, **Rehearse the next week**
  fights the next seven days ahead of time with your orders as they stand: the map shades what you
  would take in green and lose in red, and the panel lists the towns taken and lost, troops cut
  off, and each side's strength before and after. Nothing is written, and the next turn's story can
  still change it.
- **Under way.** Movements time has already passed over. Their arrows are off the map; point at one
  to see what is left of it.
- **Allies.** Each country fighting beside you commands its own troops here. **Talk strategy with
  its leader** shuts the War Room and opens a conversation with them.
- **Transporters.** Yours, with where each is bound and how it is going (**Call it off** before it
  sets out), and theirs on the way, with **Go after it** to send your forces to intercept it.
- **Fleets** and **Fortified lines**, as they stand.
- **What is known of theirs.** What the difficulty gives them here, who leads them, and what your
  spies and aircraft have found. An agent inside one of their countries sends their staff's plan and
  their planned movements; your aircraft over the front see where their troops are heading.

At the foot, **talk to your generals**. Tell them what you want done in your own words; they answer
from the real battlefield, may push back, and carry out an order they accept when time next
passes. Each message is one AI request.

## Orders from outside the War Room

You do not have to open it. An order you type as an action, such as *"the northern army digs in and
the south pushes for the city"*, reaches your troops in the same time skip: the skip fights the
period again with your order before writing its story, which costs one more request only in a turn
where your actions command troops. The story also gives orders to the sides nobody plays, can call
up more troops through drafts and reinforcements, and can send troops by sea, rail or air.

A side nobody plays has a commander of its own. It looks several plans ahead on a copy of the
battle and picks the best, weighing ground, towns, capitals, its strength, its supply and troops
nearly cut off; a deep thrust into its land is answered with a counter-offensive. A front a
scenario began with keeps the stance its author gave it.

## Difficulty

The difficulty weighs a battlefield only when a person fights on one side of it, and only the side
facing that person; a war between countries nobody plays, or between players, is fought on even
terms. The War Room says what it gives them.

| Level | Their troops fight at | Advance at | Reserves | Recover at | Their commander |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Very Easy | 85% | 90% | 70% | 75% | a plainer one, who looks less far ahead |
| Easy | 93% | 95% | 85% | 90% | a plainer one |
| Medium | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% | the best there is |
| Hard | 115% | 110% | 130% | 125% | the best, weighing how you are likely to answer |
| Very Hard | 135% | 120% | 175% | 160% | the same |
| Impossible | 175% | 140% | 300% | 250% | the same |

A side short of supplies, under a blockade that has stood for months, or past half war-weary is
weighed down on top of that. See [starting a game](/wiki/new-game/) for what else the levels change.

## Reading a battlefield

On the map a battlefield is three things. The ground each side has taken is painted in its colour
over the regions, which keep their shapes. Each troop is a counter: a red rim means it is cut off,
a yellow rim that it is advancing, a dark ring with a pale rim that it is dug in, and a worn troop
fades. Behind the troops are arrows along the way they came, merged into a few broad ones from far
off. A turn's fighting is played back as it went; after that the front skirmishes back and forth
without anything changing hands until time passes again.

On the main map the counters show only from close in. The War Room shows every troop from far off,
and so does the map while the Events panel is open, so you can see where what you are reading
happened. When an event tells of a missile strike or a bombing, the map plays it, with
interceptions; it is a picture of what the event did and changes nothing by itself.

## When a war ends

When a battlefield you fought on closes, an **after-action report** opens once: how long it was
fought, each side's formations over time, what each side took, and the turning points, such as
offensives, counter-offensives, towns and capitals taken, pockets that surrendered, sieges,
fortified lines broken through and landings. It stays to be read again under **War reports** in
the **🤝 Diplomacy** tab of your country panel (open it from your flag). How wars end on terms is on
[relations, treaties and war](/wiki/war/#ending-a-war-peace-on-terms).

## For scenario designers

The scenario editor's **Fronts** tab sets how a scenario's wars are fought: **Warfare** (fronts or
swarms, or by the date), the men a counter stands for, the mustering curve and which mechanics are
on. It also holds the fronts a scenario begins with (at war from the first day, or a fortified
border at peace, each side with its stance, its formations, its generals and whether it is
**Deployed as** a front or swarms), **Scripted outcomes** for a war meant to end one way (that side
is led by a plainer commander, and good play can still turn it), and **Rate every country** for the
countries' military figures. See [the scenario editor](/wiki/editor/#the-scenario-editor).
